In the night sky, the stars look like they are connected, as if invisible lines have always existed between them. But the lines aren't really there. We create them. We look at scattered points of light and find meaning in between the spaces.

Learning to find meaning in code felt a lot like that.

When I first started learning how to code, I thought it was mostly just a screen filled with thousands of lines of text, written by people who magically knew every line by heart.

I was wrong.

Coding isn't just a computer screen or a collection of for loops. It's about people. Every line of code is part of something bigger. The lines of commands simply being the constellation, a connection, between an idea and the person it helps.

This internship changed the way I see technology. I don't just open an app anymore. I notice the little things. Why is that button there? Why does this website feel so easy to use? Why does another one feel confusing?

I started realizing that every small detail was a choice someone made. Behind every smooth experience is a person who spent time solving problems so someone else wouldn't have to.

Programming also taught me that big problems aren't solved all at once. You solve one small piece, then another, and eventually everything comes together. It's a little like looking at the stars. At first, they seem scattered across the sky. Then you connect them, and suddenly there's a constellation. The stars never moved. You just learned how to see the bigger picture.

Not everything came easily.

There were days when one tiny mistake broke everything. Sometimes I spent longer looking for a missing semicolon than actually writing code. It was frustrating, but it also taught me something important.

Mistakes aren't the end of the project. They're part of the process.

Every bug taught me to slow down, think differently, and keep going. Looking back, I probably learned more from the things that went wrong than the things that worked the first time.
